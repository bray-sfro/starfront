How it chooses

—

Fields are only ever shot after they cross the meridian. That one rule catches each field within minutes of its transit — the highest it ever gets from here — and keeps the mount on one side of the pier all night, so a German equatorial never flips. Above good enough the extra altitude buys nothing (70° is airmass 1.06), so the survey spreads across a band of declination instead of grinding away at the zenith for a year.

Suggest builds the set a night needs: bias frames, a dark at every exposure your filters are shot at (the defaults under Equipment → Default exposure per filter), and a flat through every filter. Flats always measure their own exposure — what reaches the sensor depends on the panel, the filter and the optics, and a sky flat re-measures every frame — so there is nothing to type for one.

Every telescope shoots the set at the same time and each gets its own master, tagged with its own name. Flats have to work that way — the dust and the vignetting are the telescope's, not the mount's — and a telescope's flats are only ever given back to that telescope.

Applying them to lights

The raw frame is always kept. A calibrated copy is written beside it in a calibrated subfolder, with the masters it used named in its header.

What counts as a match

Building masters

With this on, panel brightness is only the first guess: each filter gets the brightness and exposure that put it on target together. Searching the exposure alone cannot work for luminance — it passes several times the light of any narrowband filter, so a panel set for Ha saturates L before the shutter can close. A set that names its own brightness keeps it.

The shortest flat is the one that matters on a fast telescope: a RASA under a daylit sky is at the target level in a fraction of a millisecond, and a floor set any higher just reports that the sky is too bright. Take it down to whatever your camera's own minimum is — 32 µs (0.000032 s) on most CMOS.

Sky flats

—

Tracking is off by default: nothing in a flat has to stay still, and a mount that is not tracking never crosses the meridian, so the zenith is fine on any mount. Off the meridian only matters if you turn tracking back on, when a German equatorial parked at the zenith could flip half way through and leave the second half of the frames rotated against the first.

The telescope is moved a little between every frame, so the stars land somewhere different each time and the stack combines them out. Twilight changes by a factor of two every few minutes, so every frame sets the exposure for the next one and anything that lands too far off is thrown away. The set ends when it has what it was asked for, or when the sky leaves the range the camera can follow.

The light path

Calibration shuts the cover for the darks and lights the panel for the flats. With this on, the next frame that needs sky — a sub, a focus exposure, a plate solve, or Capture on the Image tab — puts the panel out and opens the cover itself, and waits for it. Turn it off if you open the telescope by hand.

Equipment

Profile

A profile remembers every telescope here, which driver each of its slots connects to, and the settings around them — so a rig that comes apart in the spring goes back together in one click.

Your focal length, pixel size, filters and their offsets, focus and dither settings, ASTAP, and which driver is which — read from the N.I.N.A. profile you last used, shown before anything is written.

Telescopes

The master carries the mount and the guider and decides where everything points. Every other telescope is bolted to the same mount: it focuses and exposes in step with the master, and never exposes while the master is dithering.

Connect all brings up every telescope's remembered drivers — each device is remembered the first time you connect it by hand. Set a row to — none — and press Connect to leave that slot empty, so a telescope with no rotator does not report one missing every night.

ASCOM drivers appear automatically. Alpaca devices need a scan. PHD2 must be running with its server enabled (Tools → Enable Server).

Camera —

Gain and offset are applied when a sequence starts, so a night always begins from the same settings. Warming is ramped rather than switched off — a fast change can condense moisture onto the sensor window.

The cooler comes on the moment the sequence starts either way. Leave the wait off and the night gets on with homing, slewing, centring and focusing while the sensor comes down, and only pauses for temperature immediately before the first light frame — by which time there is usually nothing left to wait for. Turn it on to hold everything still until the setpoint is reached.

Camera angle —

The position angle the sensor actually sits at. Everything that has to know what shape of sky the camera covers uses it — mosaic panels and the framing rectangle. A plate solve measures it exactly, so leave the box ticked and it stays right; it is left alone while a rotator is connected, because there the angle is commanded per target rather than fixed by how the camera sits.

Filters —

Name the slots, in the order they are fitted in the wheel. Slot 1 here has to be slot 1 in the wheel: everything downstream matches on the name, so get the order wrong and the sequencer asks for Ha and moves to whatever is really in that position. Leave a slot blank if it is empty.

—

Learned from the camera's own Bayer matrix the first time it connects, and kept. Tick it yourself if the camera has not been connected yet: a collaboration needs to know before it lets a colour camera in.

For a telescope with no filter wheel — a RASA carries its filter in a drawer at prime focus, and nothing can move it. Say which one is fitted and the frames are labelled with it, the plan can allocate to it, and the sequencer says so plainly if the night asks for a different one instead of quietly shooting the wrong stack. Ignored while a wheel is connected, because then the wheel knows.

Focus offsets —

Choose this telescope's filters above first.

Filters are not parfocal: a 3 nm Ha and a luminance filter sit a few hundred steps apart on most trains. The numbers are relative to each other, not absolute positions — pick whichever filter you usually focus on, leave it at 0, and give the others how far the focuser has to move from there. A filter change then costs a second instead of a sweep.

Measure them

Focuses each filter in turn and works out the differences, then saves them here for this telescope. Costs one sweep per filter per pass, and buys every filter change afterwards.

The filters are swept round-robin and each pass is worked out against its own reading of the reference, so the temperature drift between passes cancels instead of being counted as a filter difference. Two passes is enough to see whether the numbers agree; the spread is shown below when they do not.

—

Sweeping through one filter every time keeps runs comparable and keeps the offsets above meaningful — and finds stars far faster than narrowband does. The imaging filter goes back as soon as the sweep lands. Leave it on whatever is in the path for a rig that carries one filter.

Where frames are saved

—

—

Where master darks, flats and biases live, and where the Calibrate tab builds them. Worth putting somewhere that gets backed up: a season of darks is hours of closed-shutter time that cannot be taken again on the night they turn out to be missing.

Autofocus —

The sweep extends itself: if focus turns out to be beyond the range it started with, it keeps stepping that way until both arms of the V have enough points, rather than giving up. The result is only accepted if the stars at the chosen position really are no worse than they started.

Step size is the one number that must match your focuser: the sweep should span roughly twice the range over which stars visibly bloat. Too small and the curve is flat; too large and it misses the minimum.

Backlash matters more than it looks. Every position is approached from the same direction so the sweep is consistent with itself, but the move onto the final position has to reverse — and if the overshoot is smaller than the focuser's real backlash, the confirming frame is taken somewhere the sweep never measured and the run lands short. Leave it at 0 and five step sizes are used, which covers most focusers; if the log warns that the focuser "is not going where it is told", put the real figure here.

Each telescope has its own focuser and its own sweep. These numbers belong to the one selected above; when a run needs focusing they all sweep at the same time, because a telescope in the middle of a sweep is deliberately out of focus and nothing else on the mount can be shooting anyway.

Meridian flip

A German equatorial cannot track through the meridian. Imaging stops before the target gets there, the mount is sent back to the same coordinates — which is what makes the driver flip — and the new pointing is solved and synced before imaging starts again. A fork mount reports no side of pier and is left alone whatever this says. Focus is checked after every flip, because on most rigs it shifts across one.

Slewing

How long to let the mount stop ringing before a frame is taken or a plate solve is attempted. A heavy rig on a light tripod wants more of this than a pier does.

When the sequence starts

A mount that has been power-cycled, hand-slewed, or left somewhere by another program does not know where it is pointing, and every slew after that is wrong by the same amount. Homing first costs a minute and removes the whole problem. A mount that cannot home is skipped, and one that refuses is reported without ending the night.

When the plan runs out

Both are off by default: a surprise slew in the dark is worse than a mount left tracking where you put it. Turn parking on for an observatory with a roof that has to come over the telescope. Guiding is always stopped at the end whatever these say, and the cameras warm in the background rather than holding the sequence open. Run on loop ignores both: on loop every night ends with the cover closed, the mount homed and parked, the roof shut and the cameras warm, whatever is ticked here.

Run on loop

On loop, each night opens this long before astronomical dusk so the cameras cool, the mount homes and the first focus happens in twilight, and the first frame is taken in the dark. A target with a start time still waits for it.

The weather

A safety monitor aggregates cloud, rain, wind and whatever else the observatory cares about into one answer. This is the only setting group in the program that exists to protect the equipment rather than the data.

That last one matters more than it looks. A monitor that has been unplugged, whose driver has thrown, or which has simply stopped answering is not evidence of good weather. Turning it off is a decision to trust the sky over the sensor.

Shut down for a roof this program controls. Hold for a dome somebody else closes, where the run should pick up again in twenty minutes without a slew.

A cloud sensor flickering unsafe for one reading is not weather. Coming back is deliberately much slower: starting up into a gap in the cloud is how a rig ends up opening and closing its roof all night.

Telling somebody who is not in the room

A rig at a remote site fails silently by default: the sequence stops at one in the morning and nobody reads the log until breakfast, by which time the night and the weather have both gone.

Discord, or any webhook

Discord: in your server, open the channel's settings → Integrations → Webhooks → New Webhook → Copy Webhook URL, and paste it here. Messages arrive as coloured cards with the telescope's name on them, so a club channel with several rigs posting into it still reads. Press Send a test message to prove it before a night depends on it.

Slack, Telegram, Pushover, ntfy and anything self-hosted also accept a URL to POST JSON to. Only the key the message goes under varies: text for Slack, message for ntfy and Pushover. Discord ignores this field.

Mail

What is worth interrupting somebody for

The gap only holds back failure, recovery and weather messages, so a rig failing in a loop at three in the morning does not empty a phone. A night's activity is a handful of messages spaced by the sky and is never held back.

The camera watching the telescope

Connect it on the Equipment rows like any other device — it is an ordinary ASCOM or ZWO camera in a different job, so every backend already knows how to drive it. The live view is the Pier cam button in the top bar.

Auto is the whole point of the slot. This camera has to cope with afternoon sun, dusk, moonlight and a pitch-dark dome on the same night with nobody awake to adjust it, so it meters each frame and corrects towards a target brightness. Exposure moves first because it costs nothing in noise; gain moves only when the exposure has run out of room.

The longest exposure also sets how slow the feed can get: eight seconds for one picture of a dark dome is still a live view, eighty is not. A gain ceiling is worth setting — the top of an ASI's range is unusable noise, and an auto-gain loop with nothing to stop it will find its way there on the first properly dark night.

PHD2

—

Guiding

Without this nothing ever tells PHD2 to guide: dithering between subs checks that guiding is running and quietly does nothing when it is not, so a whole night can run unguided.

Dithering

When something goes wrong

The things that go wrong at three in the morning are not exotic: the guide star goes behind a cloud, the mount drifts until the object is halfway out of frame, a camera misses a download. Each is noticed between frames, named on screen while it is being fixed, and retried a bounded number of times — the bound being the important half, because a rig that needs rescuing every five minutes has a fault that another retry will not mend.

The guide star

PHD2 usually finds the star again itself within an exposure or two, so the wait is not politeness — a cloud is over before a re-acquisition would have finished. After that the guider is stopped, given a fresh star and restarted; the later attempts recalibrate as well, which is slow and is the only thing that fixes a mount that has been nudged by hand. If none of it works the night carries on unguided and says so loudly, because an unguided sub of a bright target is still a sub.

Nothing is ever deleted. Frames taken while the guider had no lock are always flagged on the sub chart; this also moves them out of the way so they are not stacked by accident.

The pointing

Measured by the pointing check on the Monitoring page, so that has to be switched on for this to have anything to act on. The warning threshold there is for things worth knowing about; this one is for a target on its way out of the frame.

The camera

A camera that misses one download is fine; one that has fallen off the USB bus is not, and asking again is the only thing that tells them apart. Only the telescope that failed is retried, so a working camera never shoots the same slot twice.

Giving up

The budget is per target, so a night that needed three rescues on M31 is still willing to rescue NGC 7000. Set it to 0 for no limit.

Watching the run

Pointing checks run in the background so they never hold up the next exposure. Set the interval to 0 to switch them off. Recovery uses this measurement to decide when the target has drifted far enough to go back to, so leaving it off leaves that with nothing to act on.

What the rig costs between exposures

Every plan is arithmetic on two numbers: how long the shutter is open, and everything else. The first is exact. The second used to be three typed guesses, and guesses are always wrong in the same direction — nobody types in the cost of the thing they forgot about. A 61-megapixel camera on USB 2 takes eighteen seconds to hand over a frame; a nine-point autofocus sweep is four minutes, and the old arithmetic costed it at nothing at all. A night planned on those guesses runs out of dark an hour early.

Calibrate

The rig times itself as it works — every download, every filter change, every slew and every focus run — so after one night it knows. This does the same on purpose, in about the time one autofocus sweep takes: a few short frames for the download, the wheel back and forth for a filter change, and one sweep.

One sweep is enough for every filter. What gets measured is the cost per sweep point of moving the focuser and measuring the stars, which does not depend on the filter or the sub length — the exposures are arithmetic. So a nine-point sweep at six seconds and a fifteen-point sweep at twenty are both predicted from the same pair of numbers.

Slew time is not measured here: how long a slew takes depends entirely on how far it is, and a made-up slew would measure a made-up distance. That one is learned from the real slews a sequence makes.

New all-sky survey

The grid

The overlap grows towards the poles on its own. A field covers more degrees of right ascension the nearer the pole it sits, so the spacing is worked out from the edge of the field nearest the equator — which makes the number above a floor that holds everywhere, rather than an average with gaps under it. Using one spacing for the whole sky would shoot the polar rings many times over for no more coverage.

The field size is the smallest of the telescopes on the mount: it is the only one all of them can deliver, so the grid holds whichever scope a field ends up being shot on.

What that comes to

Device setup

The ProgID this telescope opens. Some drivers register one per device — ASCOM.Foo.Camera, ASCOM.Foo_2.Camera — and if yours does, giving each telescope its own here is the whole job. You can type one that is not in the dropdown.

The driver's own window opens on a thread of its own, so the rest of the program keeps running behind it. The device has to be disconnected first — a driver will not take its settings while it is in use.

Settings this telescope pins

Press Read its settings to see what the driver keeps for this ID.

These are the driver's own settings, not ours. Tick the one that names the device — a serial number, a camera index, whatever this driver calls it — and it is written into the driver in the moment before this telescope connects. That is how several telescopes share a single driver: each one says which device it means on its way up.

Site & Optics

Observing site

Longitude is degrees east of Greenwich, so the Americas are negative. The planetarium needs this to draw the horizon, the meridian and the alt/az grid.

Horizon

How low the telescope is willing to shoot. Below this the air is thick enough that the frame is rarely worth keeping, so a target is only given a window while it is above the line, and the sequencer stops a target that sinks below it mid-run rather than filling the disk with murk. Raise it if trees or a neighbour's roof take the bottom of your sky.

Optics

Focal length sizes the field-of-view rectangle and gives the plate solver its scale hint. Rotation is the camera angle, 0 = north up. The sensor is read from the camera when one is connected — fill it in here so you can plan framings with the rig switched off.

Usable field

What the optics actually correct, where that is smaller than the sensor — a RASA 8's 22 mm image circle does not reach the corners of an APS-C chip. Mosaic panels are laid out on this rather than on the full frame, so the seams are covered by glass that works. Leave both blank for a train that illuminates the whole sensor.

Plate solver

—

Centring takes a frame, solves it, slews by whatever the solve says is wrong, and does it again until the field centre is inside the tolerance or the attempts run out. A tight tolerance on a mount with backlash spends the attempts and lands no closer, so a wide field is usually better served by one arcminute than by ten arcseconds.

astrometry.net

Only ever used for a FITS frame opened as a framing reference in the Planner, never during a run: it needs the network and takes anywhere from twenty seconds to several minutes, which is fine for a planning question in the afternoon and useless at the telescope.

It earns its place on frames from somewhere else. A file off another rig — or a phone lens, or an old scan — can be at a scale this machine has no ASTAP index files for, and solving that blind is exactly what the service is built for. Your own frames almost never reach it: anything written after a plate solve already carries the answer in its header. Point Service at your own installation if you run one.

How long things take

What the planner adds to the shutter time when it works out whether a night's plan fits: download and dither between subs, the wheel moving, and the slew, settle and plate solve each mosaic panel costs. These do not drive the rig — they only decide whether the plan says the night fits, so set them to what your gear really takes and the budget stays honest.

What Auto-arrange chooses

From three things, all of which a person weighs up by hand every clear night: the Moon, what the target already has in the bank, and its goal. Everything it writes lands on the plan where you can see and change it before the night starts — it decides nothing at the telescope.

Default exposure per filter

Choose this telescope's filters in Equipment first.

Seconds per sub under a dark sky, and the number Auto-arrange reaches for first. These are starting points for a night, not limits — what actually gets shot is whatever is sitting on the plan when the sequence starts, and every one of these lands there where it can be changed.

The fallbacks are for a filter with no entry above — something unusual in the wheel still gets a sensible length rather than a default that suits nothing.

A bright Moon does not stop a night, it changes what the night is for. Narrowband barely notices it — a 3 nm filter throws away almost all of the scattered sunlight along with almost all of the sky — so above the lit fraction here a target moves onto whatever narrowband it carries. One that carries none gets shorter broadband subs instead: a bright sky fills the well sooner, and the answer to that is more shorter frames rather than fewer longer ones. Past the distance here the Moon's glow has fallen off enough that broadband is fine at any phase.

These describe the observatory, so every telescope shares them.
